Wilcoxon signed-rank test (one sample)

Purpose: non-parametric alternative to the one-sample t-test: is the median different from a hypothesised value? Example: is median pain score different from 5?

Assumptions

  • Independent observations; data at least ordinal; distribution of differences roughly symmetric.

Method

  1. Compute di = xi − m0, drop zeros.
  2. Rank |di|; V = sum of ranks of positive di.
\[E(V) = \frac{n(n+1)}{4},\quad Var(V) = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{24},\quad z = \frac{V-E(V)}{\sqrt{Var(V)}},\quad r = \frac{|z|}{\sqrt n}\]

Background

Frank Wilcoxon (1945). By using ranks instead of raw values, extreme values lose their influence, and no normality assumption is required. Exact p-values are used for small samples without ties.

Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum)

Purpose: non-parametric alternative to the independent t-test for two independent groups with skewed or ordinal data. Example: CRP in smokers vs non-smokers.

Hypotheses

H0: the two distributions are identical; i.e. P(X1 > X2) = 0.5. If the distributions have similar shape, this is a test of equal medians.

Formulae

\[U_1 = R_1 - \frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2},\qquad U = \min(U_1, n_1n_2-U_1)\] \[z = \frac{U - n_1n_2/2}{\sqrt{\frac{n_1n_2(n_1+n_2+1)}{12}}},\qquad r = \frac{|z|}{\sqrt N}\]

Background

U counts, over all n1×n2 possible pairs, how often a value from group 1 exceeds a value from group 2. U/(n1n2) is the "probability of superiority" – the same quantity as the AUC of an ROC curve. Report medians (IQR), U, p, and r (0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large).

Note: R reports W (= U1); SPSS reports the smaller U. Both give the same p-value.

Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired)

Purpose: non-parametric alternative to the paired t-test. Example: pain score before and after treatment.

Method

  1. For each subject compute the difference (after − before); drop zero differences.
  2. Rank absolute differences; give each rank the sign of its difference.
  3. V = sum of positive ranks; compare with its distribution under H0.
\[z = \frac{V - n(n+1)/4}{\sqrt{n(n+1)(2n+1)/24}},\qquad r = \frac{|z|}{\sqrt n}\]

Background

Unlike the simpler sign test (which only counts + and −), the signed-rank test also uses the size of the changes, so it is more powerful. Hodges-Lehmann estimate = median of all pairwise averages of differences, a robust estimate of the typical change.

Kruskal-Wallis H test

Purpose: non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA for 3+ independent groups.

Formula

\[H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{R_j^2}{n_j} - 3(N+1)\quad\sim\chi^2_{k-1},\qquad \varepsilon^2 = \frac{H}{N-1}\]

(divided by a tie-correction factor \(1-\sum(t^3-t)/(N^3-N)\)).

Post-hoc: Dunn's test

\[z_{ij} = \frac{\bar R_i - \bar R_j}{\sqrt{\left[\frac{N(N+1)}{12} - \frac{\sum(t^3-t)}{12(N-1)}\right]\left(\frac1{n_i}+\frac1{n_j}\right)}}\]

p-values adjusted with the Holm method. Dunn's test uses the same pooled ranks as Kruskal-Wallis, which is why it is preferred over separate Mann-Whitney tests.

Friedman test

Purpose: non-parametric alternative to repeated-measures ANOVA (same subjects, 3+ conditions / time points).

Formula

\[\chi^2_F = \frac{12}{nk(k+1)}\sum_{j=1}^k R_j^2 - 3n(k+1),\qquad W = \frac{\chi^2_F}{n(k-1)}\]

Ranks are assigned within each subject (row). Rj is the sum of ranks for condition j. Kendall's W (0-1) is the effect size. Post-hoc: pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction.

Chi-square test of independence

Purpose: test association between two categorical variables. Example: is improvement (Yes/No) associated with drug group?

Hypotheses

H0: the variables are independent.

Assumptions

  • Each subject counted once (independent).
  • Expected count ≥ 5 in at least 80% of cells and none < 1. Otherwise use Fisher's exact test.

Formulae

\[E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \times C_j}{N},\qquad \chi^2 = \sum\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}},\qquad df = (r-1)(c-1)\] \[V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N(\min(r,c)-1)}}\quad\text{(Cram\'er's V)}\]

Background

Karl Pearson (1900). If two variables are independent, the expected count in each cell follows from the margins. The chi-square statistic adds up squared deviations between observed and expected counts, scaled by expected counts. Standardised residuals (> |1.96|) show which cells drive the association. Yates' continuity correction (2×2) is conservative and many statisticians prefer the uncorrected test or Fisher's test.

Chi-square goodness-of-fit

Purpose: does the distribution of one categorical variable match an expected distribution? Examples: are blood groups distributed as in the Indian population? Are births equal across weekdays?

\[\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^k\frac{(O_i - Np_i)^2}{Np_i},\qquad df = k-1\]

Enter expected proportions in the same order as categories (alphabetical), e.g. "0.37, 0.32, 0.22, 0.09". Leave blank to test equal proportions.

Fisher's exact test

Purpose: test association in contingency tables when samples are small (expected counts < 5).

\[P = \frac{(a+b)!\,(c+d)!\,(a+c)!\,(b+d)!}{N!\;a!\;b!\;c!\;d!}\]

Background

R.A. Fisher (the "lady tasting tea" experiment). With row and column totals fixed, the probability of each possible table follows the hypergeometric distribution. The p-value is the total probability of all tables as or less probable than the observed one. It is exact – no large-sample approximation. For large R×C tables, a Monte-Carlo p-value is computed.

\[OR = \frac{ad}{bc},\quad SE(\ln OR) = \sqrt{\tfrac1a+\tfrac1b+\tfrac1c+\tfrac1d}\] \[RR = \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)},\quad SE(\ln RR) = \sqrt{\tfrac1a-\tfrac1{a+b}+\tfrac1c-\tfrac1{c+d}}\] \[ARR = R_1 - R_0,\qquad NNT = \frac{1}{|ARR|},\qquad 95\%\,CI = e^{\ln(\text{ratio})\pm1.96\,SE}\]

Which to use?

  • Cohort studies / RCTs: RR (risk can be measured directly), ARR and NNT.
  • Case-control studies: OR only (risk cannot be estimated because cases are sampled).
  • OR ≈ RR when the outcome is rare (< 10%).

RR or OR = 1 means no association; a CI including 1 means not significant. NNT = number of patients to treat to prevent one bad outcome.

One-sample proportion test

Purpose: compare an observed proportion with a known value. Example: is the smoking prevalence in our sample different from the national 25%?

\[z = \frac{\hat p - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}\]

The exact binomial test sums binomial probabilities and is preferred for small n.

Two-proportion z-test

Purpose: compare a proportion between two independent groups (e.g. % improved in males vs females).

\[\bar p = \frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2},\qquad z = \frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_2}{\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)\left(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}\right)}}\]

Equivalent to the 2×2 chi-square test (z² = χ²). The CI of the difference is reported.

Pearson correlation coefficient (r)

Purpose: measure the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two numeric variables. Example: SBP by mercury sphygmomanometer vs digital device.

\[r = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}},\qquad t = \frac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}},\; df = n-2\]

Interpretation

−1 to +1. |r| < 0.3 weak, 0.3-0.5 moderate, 0.5-0.7 strong, > 0.7 very strong. r² = proportion of shared variance.

Assumptions & caveats

  • Linear relation; both approximately normal; no major outliers (one outlier can create or destroy a correlation).
  • Correlation ≠ causation.
  • Correlation ≠ agreement: two methods can correlate perfectly yet differ by 20 mmHg. Use Bland-Altman for method comparison.

CI uses Fisher's z-transformation \(z = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}\), SE = 1/√(n−3).

Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) and Kendall's τ

Purpose: measure monotonic association when data are skewed, ordinal, or contain outliers.

\[\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}\quad(\text{no ties}),\qquad \tau = \frac{n_c - n_d}{\tfrac12 n(n-1)}\]

di = difference between the ranks of xi and yi. Spearman = Pearson correlation applied to ranks. Kendall's τ counts concordant vs discordant pairs; it is more robust for small samples with many ties.

Correlation matrix

Computes pairwise correlations among many variables at once and displays a heat-map. Useful for exploring data and checking multicollinearity before regression. Beware of multiple testing: with k variables there are k(k−1)/2 tests.

Linear regression (simple and multiple)

Purpose: predict a numeric outcome from one or more predictors and estimate the independent effect of each predictor, adjusting for others (confounders).

Model

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \dots + \beta_kX_k + \varepsilon,\qquad \hat\beta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty\] Simple regression: \[b_1 = \frac{\sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)}{\sum(x-\bar x)^2},\quad b_0 = \bar y - b_1\bar x\] \[R^2 = 1-\frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}},\qquad R^2_{adj} = 1-(1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}\]

Interpretation

  • B: change in Y per 1-unit increase in X, holding other predictors constant. For a categorical predictor: difference from the reference (first) category.
  • Standardised β: allows comparison of predictors measured in different units.
  • R²: proportion of variance in Y explained.

Assumptions (LINE)

  • Linearity, Independence, Normal residuals, Equal variance (homoscedasticity) – check the diagnostic plots.
  • No severe multicollinearity (VIF < 5-10).
  • Rule of thumb: at least 10-15 observations per predictor.

Background

"Regression" was named by Francis Galton (1886) who noticed that children of very tall parents were tall but closer to average – "regression to the mean". The method of least squares (Legendre, Gauss) finds the line minimising the sum of squared vertical distances.

Binary logistic regression

Purpose: model a binary outcome (died / survived, complication yes / no) from several predictors, giving adjusted odds ratios.

\[\ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1+\dots+\beta_kX_k,\qquad p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\dots)}}\] \[OR_j = e^{\beta_j},\qquad 95\%\,CI = e^{\beta_j\pm1.96\,SE}\]

Why not linear regression?

A probability must stay between 0 and 1; the logit (log-odds) transformation maps it onto the whole real line, where a linear model works.

Model checks

  • Likelihood-ratio test: is the model better than no predictors?
  • Nagelkerke R²: pseudo-R² (0-1).
  • Hosmer-Lemeshow test: calibration; p > 0.05 = good fit.
  • AUC (C-statistic): discrimination.
  • Events per variable (EPV) ≥ 10: otherwise estimates are unreliable.

Coefficients are estimated by maximum likelihood (iteratively reweighted least squares).

Cohen's kappa

Purpose: agreement between two raters / methods for a categorical rating, corrected for chance. Example: two radiologists grading X-rays as mild/moderate/severe.

\[\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1-P_e},\qquad P_e = \sum_i p_{i\cdot}\,p_{\cdot i}\]

Weighted kappa (linear or quadratic weights) gives partial credit for near-agreement in ordered categories; quadratic weighted kappa is mathematically equivalent to the ICC. Landis & Koch: ≤0.2 slight, 0.21-0.4 fair, 0.41-0.6 moderate, 0.61-0.8 substantial, >0.8 almost perfect. SE by Fleiss-Cohen-Everitt (1969).

Bland-Altman analysis

Purpose: assess agreement between two methods measuring the same numeric quantity (e.g. a new digital BP device vs mercury sphygmomanometer; glucometer vs lab glucose).

\[\text{bias} = \bar d,\qquad LoA = \bar d \pm 1.96\,s_d\]

Background

J.M. Bland & D.G. Altman (Lancet, 1986) showed that correlation is inappropriate for method comparison: correlation measures association, not agreement, and depends on the range of values. Plotting differences against averages shows the bias and the range within which 95% of differences lie. Whether the limits are clinically acceptable must be decided a priori from clinical knowledge.

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)

Purpose: reliability / agreement of numeric measurements by 2+ raters or repeated measurements (test-retest).

\[ICC(2,1) = \frac{MS_R - MS_E}{MS_R + (k-1)MS_E + \frac{k}{n}(MS_C - MS_E)},\qquad ICC(3,1) = \frac{MS_R - MS_E}{MS_R+(k-1)MS_E}\]

MSR = between-subjects mean square, MSC = between-raters, MSE = error (two-way ANOVA). Shrout & Fleiss (1979) / McGraw & Wong (1996) forms. Koo & Li (2016): <0.5 poor, 0.5-0.75 moderate, 0.75-0.9 good, >0.9 excellent. Report the ICC form, e.g. "ICC(2,1), two-way random, absolute agreement".

ROC curve and AUC

Purpose: evaluate a numeric diagnostic marker across all possible cut-offs and choose the best cut-off. Example: procalcitonin for sepsis.

The ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every cut-off. The area under the curve (AUC) = probability that a randomly chosen diseased patient has a higher marker value than a randomly chosen non-diseased patient.

\[AUC = \frac{U}{n_1 n_0},\qquad J = Sens + Spec - 1\]

AUC 0.5 = useless (coin toss), 0.7-0.8 acceptable, 0.8-0.9 good, >0.9 excellent. The SE follows Hanley & McNeil (1982). Youden's J identifies the cut-off maximising Sens + Spec. Background: ROC analysis originated in World War II radar signal detection.

Cronbach's alpha

Purpose: internal consistency of a questionnaire / scale – do the items measure the same construct?

\[\alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1-\frac{\sum s_i^2}{s_T^2}\right)\]

k = number of items, si² = item variances, sT² = variance of total score. ≥ 0.7 acceptable, ≥ 0.8 good, ≥ 0.9 excellent (> 0.95 may mean redundant items). Recode reverse-worded items first. Items with corrected item-total correlation < 0.3 are candidates for removal.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank test

Purpose: estimate the probability of surviving (remaining event-free) over time when follow-up is incomplete (censored) and compare groups.

Data required

  • Time: from start (diagnosis, randomisation) to event or last follow-up.
  • Status: 1 = event occurred (death, relapse), 0 = censored.
\[\hat S(t) = \prod_{t_i\le t}\left(1-\frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)\]

ni = number at risk just before ti, di = events at ti. Greenwood's formula gives the variance.

Log-rank test

\[\chi^2 = \frac{(O_1-E_1)^2}{Var(O_1-E_1)},\qquad E_{1i} = d_i\frac{n_{1i}}{n_i}\]

Compares observed events with those expected if survival were identical, summing over all event times. Assumes censoring is non-informative and hazards roughly proportional.

Background

Kaplan & Meier (1958) – one of the most cited statistics papers ever. Simply calculating "% died" ignores time and loses information from censored subjects.

Cox proportional hazards regression

Purpose: assess the effect of several predictors on survival time, giving adjusted hazard ratios.

\[h(t\mid X) = h_0(t)\exp(\beta_1X_1+\dots+\beta_kX_k),\qquad HR = e^{\beta}\]

HR > 1: higher hazard (worse survival); HR < 1: protective. The baseline hazard h0(t) is not estimated (semi-parametric) – Sir David Cox's (1972) key insight via partial likelihood.

Assumption

Proportional hazards: the HR is constant over time (KM curves should not cross). Tested with Schoenfeld residuals (p < 0.05 suggests violation). C-index (concordance) measures discrimination like AUC.

Sample size for comparing two means

\[n = \frac{2\,(Z_{1-\alpha/2}+Z_{1-\beta})^2\,\sigma^2}{\Delta^2}\quad\text{per group}\qquad\text{(paired: } n = \frac{(Z_{1-\alpha/2}+Z_{1-\beta})^2\sigma_d^2}{\Delta^2}\text{)}\]

Z1−α/2 = 1.96 for α = 0.05; Z1−β = 0.84 for 80% power, 1.28 for 90%. σ from a pilot study or published literature; Δ = minimum clinically important difference (decided clinically, not statistically). The exact method uses the t distribution (R power.t.test). Always inflate for dropout: nfinal = n / (1 − dropout).

Why calculate sample size?

Too small a study may miss a real effect (type II error) – an unethical waste of patients' participation. Ethics committees and the NMC thesis guidelines require a justified sample size.

Sample size for comparing two proportions

\[n = \frac{\left[Z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)} + Z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)+p_2(1-p_2)}\right]^2}{(p_1-p_2)^2}\]

p1, p2 = expected proportions with the outcome in each group (from literature / pilot). Smaller differences need much larger samples.

Sample size to estimate a prevalence

\[n = \frac{Z_{1-\alpha/2}^2\,p(1-p)}{d^2}\qquad\text{Finite population: } n' = \frac{n}{1+\frac{n-1}{N}}\]

p = expected prevalence, d = absolute precision (margin of error). Example: p = 20%, d = 5% → n = 1.96²×0.2×0.8/0.05² = 246. For cluster sampling multiply by the design effect (often 1.5-2). If p is unknown, use 0.5 (maximum n). Commonly used in community medicine cross-sectional studies.

Sample size to detect a correlation

\[C = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r},\qquad n = \left(\frac{Z_{1-\alpha/2}+Z_{1-\beta}}{C}\right)^2 + 3\]

Sample size for one-way ANOVA

Based on Cohen's effect size \(f = \sigma_{means}/\sigma\) (0.1 small, 0.25 medium, 0.4 large) and the non-central F distribution (R power.anova.test). Enter expected group means and the common within-group SD.
